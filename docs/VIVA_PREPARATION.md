### Category 1: Project Motivation & Problem Statement
#### Q1: What is the primary problem that GraphGuard aims to solve?
**Answer:** Traditional Network Intrusion Detection Systems (NIDS) primarily utilize signature matching (which fails against novel attack mutations) or point-based tabular machine learning (such as Random Forest or XGBoost). Point-based ML evaluates each network flow in complete topological isolation, ignoring the relational structure, host communication roles, and spatial network context. GraphGuard solves this by formulating intrusion detection as an inductive edge-classification task over a dynamic property graph, fusing local flow traffic statistics with higher-order topological graph embeddings to detect intrusions with high recall and minimal false positives.

#### Q2: Why is intrusion detection considered challenging in enterprise environments?
**Answer:** Enterprise network traffic is characterized by extreme class imbalance (legitimate traffic often comprises over 97–99% of total volume), massive packet velocity, dynamic IP allocations, and multi-stage attack strategies. Furthermore, black-box ML models produce alert fatigue in Security Operations Centers (SOCs) because analysts lack forensic explanations and alert prioritization mechanisms.

### Category 2: Dataset Selection (CICIDS2017)
#### Q3: Why did you choose the CICIDS2017 benchmark over older datasets like KDD Cup 99 or NSL-KDD?
**Answer:** Datasets like KDD Cup 99 and NSL-KDD are over 25 years old and suffer from severe synthetic artifacts, redundant records, obsolete traffic protocols, and a lack of realistic modern attack signatures. CICIDS2017, generated by the Canadian Institute for Cybersecurity, provides realistic background traffic generated using the B-Profile system, modern network protocols (HTTP, HTTPS, SSH, FTP), complete bidirectional PCAP captures, and extracted 5-tuple network flow features generated via CICFlowMeter.

#### Q4: What specific portion of CICIDS2017 is evaluated in this work?
**Answer:** We evaluated the complete **Tuesday, July 4, 2017** capture (`Tuesday-WorkingHours.pcap_ISCX.csv`). This capture contains 445,645 clean network flows featuring legitimate background activity alongside two distinct, deliberate brute-force attack campaigns: `FTP-Patator` (morning campaign) and `SSH-Patator` (afternoon campaign).

### Category 3: Why Graph Neural Networks (GNNs)?
#### Q5: Why are Graph Neural Networks fundamentally better suited for network intrusion detection than traditional ML?
**Answer:** Computer networks are physical and logical graphs. An attacker rarely compromises a system in a single isolated packet; attacks involve reconnaissance, repetitive queries, scanning, and pivoting. Traditional ML treats each row in a CSV as an independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) sample, which violates reality. GNNs capture relational dependencies via message-passing: a node's representation is recursively computed from the features of its communicating neighbors, allowing the model to learn structural anomalies (e.g., a node with zero ingress suddenly fanning out to multiple authentication ports).

#### Q6: Why not use a standard Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) or Recurrent Neural Network (RNN)?
**Answer:** CNNs operate on Euclidean grid-structured data (like images with fixed 2D pixel grids), and RNNs operate on linear sequential data (like natural text or time series). Network topology is non-Euclidean: a router or server can connect to dozens or thousands of endpoints, with irregular degree distributions and permutation-invariant neighborhoods. GNNs are designed specifically to operate on arbitrary, irregular graph topologies.

### Category 4: Why GraphSAGE?
#### Q7: Why did you select GraphSAGE rather than standard Graph Convolutional Networks (GCN) or Graph Attention Networks (GAT)?
**Answer:** Standard GCNs require full-batch Laplacian computation over the entire graph adjacency matrix $\mathbf{A}$, making them transductive and computationally prohibitive for large, dynamic graphs where new nodes or edges arrive at runtime. GAT introduces self-attention across edges, which has quadratic memory overhead per neighborhood. **GraphSAGE (Sample and Aggregate)** learns parameterized neighborhood aggregation functions (e.g., mean aggregation) rather than transductive node-specific embeddings. This enables true **inductive generalization** to unseen nodes and edges without retraining.

#### Q8: What aggregation function does your GraphSAGE implementation use?
**Answer:** Our implementation uses the **Mean Aggregator**:
$$\mathbf{h}_{\mathcal{N}(v)}^{(k)} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{N}(v)|} \sum_{u \in \mathcal{N}(v)} \mathbf{h}_u^{(k-1)}$$
Mean aggregation is computationally efficient ($O(|\mathcal{E}|)$), preserves scale-invariant neighborhood statistics, and integrates seamlessly with PyTorch Geometric's `SAGEConv` operator.

### Category 5 & 6: Graph Formulation (Nodes as IPs, Edges as Flows)
#### Q9: Why are nodes defined as IP addresses rather than MAC addresses or ports?
**Answer:** IP addresses represent the primary routing and endpoint identity layer across Layer-3 network communications. In enterprise environments and CICIDS2017, IP addresses define the communicating entities (clients, servers, attackers). Ports are transport-layer parameters and are appropriately encoded into the edge traffic feature vectors.

#### Q10: Why are network flows modeled as edges rather than nodes?
**Answer:** A flow is a directional communication transaction from a Source IP to a Destination IP. Modeling flows as directed edges preserves natural network semantics: endpoints are vertices ($u, v$), and the communication interaction is the link $e = (u, v)$. This enables flow classification to be framed as **edge classification**, where predictions directly map to specific network connections.

### Category 7 & 8: Adaptive Graph Construction (AGC)
#### Q11: What is Adaptive Graph Construction (AGC) and what problem does it solve?
**Answer:** In long-running network monitoring, a static graph accumulates every connection that ever occurred, creating an overly dense, noisy "hairball" graph where stale links dilute GNN message passing. AGC partitions traffic into sliding temporal windows (10 minutes), tracks edge activity using an Exponential Moving Average (EMA) weight decay ($\lambda = 0.95$), reinforces active communication channels, and prunes edges falling below a minimum weight threshold ($w < 0.01$). This maintains a compact, temporally active representation of the network.

#### Q12: Is AGC used during test inference in GraphGuard?
**Answer:** AGC processes historical traffic windows up to the validation cutoff to build the dynamic historical context graph $G_{\text{hist}}$. AGC operates strictly on timestamps and flow frequencies without labels, ensuring zero target label leakage.

### Category 9 & 10: Baseline Model Selection (Random Forest & XGBoost)
#### Q13: Why did you choose Random Forest and XGBoost as the primary baselines?
**Answer:** In contemporary NIDS academic literature and industrial benchmark competitions, Random Forest and Gradient Boosted Decision Trees (XGBoost) represent the gold-standard state-of-the-art tabular classifiers. They handle non-linear decision boundaries, feature colinearities, and mixed-scale continuous features exceptionally well. Evaluating GraphGuard against these strong baselines provides an honest, competitive benchmark.

#### Q14: How were the baselines configured to ensure a fair comparison?

Random Forest used 200 unpruned trees with balanced class weighting. XGBoost used 200 estimators, max depth 6, and a learning rate of 0.10. Both models were trained and evaluated independently.

### Category 11: Edge Classification Architecture
#### Q15: How does GraphGuard perform edge classification from node embeddings?
**Answer:** For an edge $(u, v)$ with raw traffic feature vector $\mathbf{e}_{uv} \in \mathbb{R}^{77}$, the GraphSAGE encoder produces source node embedding $\mathbf{h}_u \in \mathbb{R}^{64}$ and destination node embedding $\mathbf{h}_v \in \mathbb{R}^{64}$. GraphGuard concatenates them into a unified representation:
$$\mathbf{z}_{uv} = [\mathbf{h}_u \,\|\, \mathbf{h}_v \,\|\, \mathbf{e}_{uv}] \in \mathbb{R}^{205}$$
This 205-dimensional vector is passed through an MLP classifier with two hidden layers ($256 \to 128$) with Batch Normalization, ReLU activations, and Dropout ($p=0.30$) before projecting to the output class logits.

#### Q16: Why concatenate node embeddings with edge features instead of using only edge features or only node embeddings?
**Answer:** Node embeddings $\mathbf{h}_u, \mathbf{h}_v$ encode the topological role and communication context of the source and destination (e.g., whether the host is an authentication hub or an isolated client). Edge features $\mathbf{e}_{uv}$ encode the physical flow telemetry (packet sizes, timing, flags). Fusing both allows the classifier to correlate *who is communicating* with *what is being transmitted*.

### Category 12 & 13: Explainable AI (XAI) & SHAP
#### Q17: Why is Explainable AI necessary in an intrusion detection system?
**Answer:** Deep learning models are non-linear black boxes. In enterprise cybersecurity, an automated block action can disrupt mission-critical business operations. Security analysts cannot blindly trust a high classification score without understanding the underlying technical justification. XAI provides forensic evidence showing which packet features caused the alarm.

#### Q18: How is SHAP implemented in GraphGuard?
**Answer:** Full GNNs with recursive message passing cannot be directly explained by standard Shapley algorithms without exponential cost. GraphGuard employs a surrogate explainability approach: a multi-threaded `RandomForestClassifier` surrogate model is fitted on a representative background sample of training traffic features and model predictions. We then apply `shap.TreeExplainer` to compute exact Shapley feature attributions for individual suspicious test flows, generating human-interpretable waterfall charts.

#### Q19: What specific features did SHAP identify as the primary drivers of brute-force attacks in your test set?
**Answer:** Across the 20 explained attack flows, the top features were:
1. `Fwd Packet Length Mean` (small, fixed payload size during authentication queries).
2. `Max Packet Length` (absence of substantive data payloads).
3. `Average Packet Size` (highly asymmetric request-response profile).
4. `Fwd PSH Flags` and `SYN Flag Count` (rapid socket handshake initialization).
5. `Destination Port` (port 21 for FTP, port 22 for SSH).

### Category 14: Threat Propagation Score (TPS)
#### Q20: What is the Threat Propagation Score (TPS) and how is it calculated?
**Answer:** TPS is a composite alert-prioritization metric in $[0, 1]$ computed for each IP node in the graph:
$$\text{TPS}(u) = 0.35 \cdot \text{AttProb}(u) + 0.25 \cdot \text{SuspNeigh}(u) + 0.20 \cdot \text{RecFreq}(u) + 0.10 \cdot \text{DegCent}(u) + 0.10 \cdot \text{HubScore}(u)$$
Where components measure outbound attack flow fraction, ratio of compromised neighbors, recent 30-minute attack frequency, normalized degree centrality, and hub betweenness proxy.

#### Q21: What threat tiers exist, and what were the TPS results on your test graph?
**Answer:** TPS defines 4 tiers: CRITICAL ($\ge 0.75$), HIGH ($\ge 0.50$), MEDIUM ($\ge 0.25$), and LOW ($< 0.25$). On the 5,728 scored nodes, TPS correctly identified:
- Node `172.16.0.1` as **CRITICAL (TPS = 1.0000)** — The exact external attacker machine executing Patator brute force.
- Node `52.35.227.152` as **MEDIUM (TPS = 0.3368)** — The targeted server receiving automated credential queries.
- 5,726 remaining nodes as **LOW** (average TPS = 0.0107).

#### Q22: Does TPS claim to predict which IP will attack in the future?
**Answer:** **No.** TPS is strictly an **alert-prioritization ranking mechanism** designed to sort current security alerts and help SOC analysts prioritize incident response. It makes no deterministic claims about future attacker behavior.

### Category 15: Class Imbalance Handling
#### Q23: How does GraphGuard handle the severe class imbalance in CICIDS2017?
**Answer:** The training set is 93.98% Benign and only 6.02% Attacks. We utilize **inverse-frequency class weighting** in the cross-entropy loss function:
$$w_c = \frac{N}{C \cdot N_c}$$
Where $N$ is total flows, $C$ is number of classes, and $N_c$ is support for class $c$. This penalizes misclassifications on minority attack classes (`SSH-Patator` and `FTP-Patator`) proportionally higher than majority benign flows, preventing the model from collapsing into a naive majority-class predictor.

### Category 16 & 17: Temporal Splitting & Data Leakage Eradication
#### Q24: What is data leakage, and what forms of data leakage were identified and fixed in GraphGuard?
**Answer:** Data leakage occurs when information from outside the training dataset (such as future events or ground-truth labels) inadvertently influences model training. Two major forms of leakage were identified and eradicated:
1. **Feature-level label leakage:** The prior implementation included `attack_neighbor_ratio` in node features, which directly used ground-truth labels. This was permanently purged and replaced with the label-free topological feature `unique_neighbors`.
2. **Duplicate flow leakage:** 4,634 validation flows and 3,570 test flows had identical feature vectors to training flows due to repeated automated retries. All feature duplicates were strictly purged from validation and test sets.

#### Q25: What is the "Campaign-Window Chronological Split"?
**Answer:** In a standard global chronological split across the entire day, early morning and afternoon brute-force campaigns were captured in Train and Validation, leaving the final 15% test window with 0 attack flows (benign-only). To resolve this, we implemented a **Campaign-Window Chronological Split**: within each attack campaign window independently, traffic is sorted chronologically and split 70% Train, 15% Validation, and 15% Held-out Test. This preserves temporal causality ($t_{\text{train}} \le t_{\text{val}} \le t_{\text{test}}$) while ensuring the final held-out test set contains **710 genuine attack flows**.

### Category 18: Inductive vs Transductive Learning
#### Q26: What is the difference between transductive and inductive graph learning?
**Answer:**
- **Transductive Learning:** The entire graph topology (including validation and test nodes/edges) is known and fixed during training. The model learns embeddings tied to specific node indices. If a new node or connection appears, the entire GNN must be retrained.
- **Inductive Learning:** The model learns parameterized aggregation functions rather than static embeddings. During inference, the model generates embeddings for new nodes or edges on the fly using only historical graph context.

#### Q27: How does GraphGuard enforce an inductive protocol with target-edge exclusion?
**Answer:** The training graph $G_{\text{train}}$ contains only training edges (155,544). During test evaluation, test flows are **never added to the graph**. Historical context $G_{\text{hist}}$ (Train + Val edges: 184,240) is used to compute node representations via GraphSAGE message passing. Each test flow $(u, v)$ is evaluated purely as a query edge $[\mathbf{h}_u \,\|\, \mathbf{h}_v \,\|\, \mathbf{e}_{uv}]$. This eliminates transductive leakage.

### Category 21 & 22: Performance Metrics (Precision, Recall, F1, FPR)
#### Q32: Why did Random Forest and XGBoost have high accuracy (0.9994) yet perform worse than GraphGuard?
**Answer:** In imbalanced datasets, accuracy is dominated by the majority class. Random Forest and XGBoost achieved 0.9994 accuracy, but their Macro Recall was only 0.9468 because they missed 18 and 16 attacks respectively (failing on minority SSH brute-force attempts). GraphGuard achieved a **Macro Recall of 0.9922** and **100% attack recall (710/710)**, yielding a superior **Macro F1 of 0.9787**.

#### Q33: Why is False Positive Rate (FPR) critically important in enterprise NIDS?
**Answer:** An enterprise network generates millions of flows per day. An FPR of 1% (like Random Forest's 0.0127) generates 10,000 false alarms for every 1,000,000 flows, completely overwhelming SOC analysts. GraphGuard's FPR of **0.0003 (0.03%)** represents a **28x to 31x reduction in false alarms**, ensuring high operational viability.

### Category 23 & 24: Limitations & Future Work
#### Q34: What are the primary technical limitations of the current GraphGuard implementation?
**Answer:**
1. **Attack Scope:** Current evaluation focuses on credential brute-force attacks (`FTP-Patator` and `SSH-Patator`). Evaluating volumetric DDoS, botnet infiltration, and multi-day lateral movement requires expanding to Wednesday–Friday captures.
2. **Computational Overhead:** Inductive neighborhood aggregation requires higher memory bandwidth than tabular decision trees.
3. **Dynamic IP Churn:** In high-churn DHCP environments, IP node identities require periodic mapping to MAC addresses or host certificates.

#### Q35: How can GraphGuard be extended in future research?
**Answer:**
1. **Heterogeneous Graphs:** Modeling distinct node types (IPs, Ports, Protocols, Domains) and edge types.
2. **Temporal Edge Networks (TGN):** Incorporating continuous-time dynamic graph networks with continuous memory modules.
3. **Automated SOC Response:** Coupling TPS scores with automated firewall rule reconfigurations for self-healing networks.

### Category 25: GraphGuard vs Standard Machine Learning
#### Q36: If an examiner asks: "Can't I just add graph features (like in_degree) into Random Forest instead of using GraphSAGE?", how do you answer?
**Answer:** Adding static graph features into Random Forest gives node-level statistics, but it does **not** perform relational neighborhood representation learning. GraphSAGE iteratively aggregates non-linear transformations of neighbor feature distributions across 2 hops, allowing node representations to be dynamically informed by the structural behaviors of connected peers. Our ablation study proved that direct node+edge tabular representations without message passing (`no_graphsage`) perform sub-optimally compared to graph convolutional representations.

#### Q37: Why did Random Forest and XGBoost produce identical multiclass test predictions?
**Answer:** Both models were trained on the exact same 77 CICFlowMeter statistical features. In the CICIDS2017 Tuesday capture, brute-force attack features (such as `Fwd Packet Length Mean` and `Destination Port`) are sharply separable from benign traffic. While RF and XGBoost output different soft probabilities (differing by up to 0.3888), both models have sufficient margin away from the decision boundary that their `argmax` class assignments coincide across the 29,762 test instances. In binary classification, where probability calibration near the 0.5 decision boundary governs predictions, the two models diverged on 2 borderline flows.

#### Q38: How does GraphGuard prevent overfitting?
**Answer:** Overfitting is mitigated through:
1. Dropout ($p=0.30$) applied after every SAGEConv and MLP dense layer.
2. Batch Normalization applied across all hidden layers.
3. L2 weight decay ($5 \times 10^{-4}$) in the Adam optimizer.
4. Early Stopping with a patience of 15 epochs monitored strictly on validation Macro F1.

#### Q39: What loss function is used, and why?
**Answer:** Weighted Cross-Entropy Loss:
$$\mathcal{L} = - \sum_{c=1}^C w_c \cdot y_c \log(\hat{y}_c)$$
Where $w_c$ are inverse-frequency class weights computed on training traffic to balance gradient contributions across benign flows and minority attack flows.

#### Q40: What hardware accelerator backend was used for training?
**Answer:** GraphGuard supports native Apple Silicon GPU acceleration using PyTorch's Metal Performance Shaders (`mps`) backend, with automated fallback to CUDA on Linux or CPU. Each training epoch completes in approximately 4.5–5.5 seconds.
